Our Verdict False
False
Warm water has no antiviral properties. While drinking warm water can soothe a sore throat and aid hydration, it cannot kill viruses or cure any viral infection. This has been confirmed by the WHO, the CDC, and multiple independent virologists.
No credible study supports this claim. Reuters reviewed the study cited in the post and found it was not conducted by the named university, not peer-reviewed, and not findable in any academic database. Independent scientists confirmed 5G operates within internationally established safety limits.
Viruses replicate inside human cells. Once inside the body, the temperature of liquids consumed has no effect on viral replication or immune response. The claim that warm water "washes" viruses into the stomach where acid kills them has also been reviewed and found to be unsupported — stomach acid does not reliably inactivate respiratory viruses, and transmission primarily occurs via respiratory droplets, not ingestion.
5G technology operates in the radio frequency spectrum, which is non-ionising radiation. The International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP) has reviewed the evidence extensively and confirmed that 5G exposure at regulated levels produces no biological effects linked to headaches or any other health condition. The "university study" cited in the post does not appear in any academic database.

Where did this claim come from?

The claim traces back to a voice note recorded in Urdu that began circulating on WhatsApp in early February 2026. The speaker identifies themselves as "Dr. Hamid" but provides no institution or credentials. No person matching this description has been identified.

The voice note was transcribed and converted into an image forward, a text message, and later a short video clip — all carrying the same underlying claim. fcheck.in received 147 submissions of this claim across WhatsApp, web, and Telegram between 3 February and 10 March 2026.

A substantively identical claim circulated during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 and was debunked by WHO, AFP, and Reuters at that time. The reappearance in 2026 suggests deliberate recycling of prior misinformation.

The claim originated from a Facebook post in January 2026 that included a fabricated graph attributed to "University of [X] Health Sciences." Reuters contacted the named institution, which confirmed no such study was conducted there.

The post was shared over 40,000 times before being flagged. It appears to have been generated using an AI image tool to produce a convincing-looking academic graph.
